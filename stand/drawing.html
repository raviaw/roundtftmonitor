This is the whole design in one view. The shell is a rounded pebble, so a plain bore would touch it on a single line and let it rock. Instead the bore is cut wider than the shell and a front lip closes in over it: the shell slides in from the back and stops when its face meets that lip. Because the cradle leans back 38°, gravity presses it onto the lip rather than out of it — no clip, no clamp, and it still lifts out by hand.

The USB-C notch is not in this cutting plane — it is cut through the rim 90° round, on the viewer's left.

The tightest fit — lip against glass

Measured, 1.5 mm to play with

The shell is 45.0 mm across and its black glass is 42.0 mm, which leaves a plastic rim just 1.5 mm wide on radius. That rim is the only thing the lip can land on, and it is the tightest fit anywhere in the part. aperture splits it: 0.5 mm clear of the glass, 1.0 mm of ledge under the shell.

FDM prints holes undersize, which moves the lip toward the glass and away from losing grip — so that 0.5 mm is a shrink budget, and the ledge only ever gains. Even at a pessimistic 0.5 mm of shrink the lip still clears the glass by 0.25 mm. And the failure is bounded: the active display is only ⌀32.4, leaving 5.3 mm of radial margin, so a lip that lands wrong makes the shell rock — it cannot cover the screen.

Design notes

Why it is shaped this way

A lip, not a cone

The first attempt seated the shell in a shallow cone, wedging it at its equator. That was abandoned: where a cone grips depends on how the shell's rim curves, which cannot be measured off a photograph, and the first test drove the shell 1208 mm³ into the cone wall. A lip does not care about the rim profile — it stops the shell in the same place whatever the rim does.

The USB-C exits sideways

The port is on the shell's rim, and a right-angle plug needs about 10 mm of radial room. At the bottom of the ring that room is below the desk, so the notch is cut through the side instead, where it costs nothing and leaves the load-bearing bottom arc of the ring intact. Rotate the UI in firmware to match.

Three faults the numbers caught

Measuring found what looking at renders did not. The base slab was filling the bottom of the pocket — the ring merges into the base, so the seat has to be cut after the base and leg are unioned on, not before. The ring's back-bottom edge hung 1.31 mm below the build plate. And the base plate itself was floating 2.25 mm off the plate, leaving the part balanced on an 18 × 8 mm bar at the back: the plate was being lifted by half its thickness as though it were centred on the origin, when it was already built up from it.

The stance is derived, not typed in

Lean the cradle further and its lowest point moves, which is how it ended up under the build plate the first time. So the cradle's height is now computed from the lean angle rather than written down beside it — change tilt alone and the geometry follows.

Printing

Print base-down as oriented, in PLA or PETG, 3 perimeters and 40% infill — the infill is for mass, so the finished stand doesn't skid when you swipe the screen. The underside of the leaning cradle is a mild overhang: at 38° it is a real overhang but still inside the 45° most printers manage unsupported. Print it with a brim and a 60° bed; the first attempt came off the plate without them.

Printing on the K1 Max

0.4 nozzle · 300³ bed

Load stand.3mf rather than the STL — 3MF carries units, so it arrives at the right size and orientation with nothing to set. It is already base-down on z = 0; don't rotate it. At 58 × 70 × 50 mm it uses 23% of the build volume.

Why no supports

Only 46 mm² of the whole part leans past 55°, and it is all one feature: the lead-in chamfer at the top-back of the ring. That chamfer is cut at 45° to the ring's axis, so once the ring leans 38° it ends up facing nearly straight down. It will droop a little, which costs nothing — the chamfer exists only to let the shell start into the bore. Supports would scar the seat instead.

It came off the bed, and the brim was only half of it

The first print let go mid-job. The filament profile inherits the generic PLA library, which runs textured PEI at 45 °C with a 200 °C nozzle — Creality's own PLA profile says 60/220. A 45 °C plate barely holds PLA, and with cooling off for only one layer the part fan hits 100% on layer 2 and peels the corners while the plate is still weak. Nothing in the slicer's interface flags this; it is visible only in the G-code.

Asking for a brim is also not the same as getting one: outer_brim_only is not a value OrcaSlicer 2.4.2 accepts, so it fell back to auto_brim, which decided this part needed none — and the re-sliced file came out identical to the one that failed. The spelling it wants is outer_only. The check now measures the brim out of the toolpaths rather than trusting the setting.

Slow the layers down

The stock profiles are built for speed and this part has a tiny cross-section per layer — up in the ring, each layer is a thin arc. At full speed those layers finish before the one beneath has set and the ring comes out soft and glossy. A minimum layer time of 8–10 s, or capping the outer wall to about 30% speed, fixes it. Printing PLA in an enclosed machine, leave the door and top open so the chamber doesn't heat-soak the extruder.

Adversarial review

Second pass, after every dimension was confirmed

Run deliberately looking for reasons this would fail. Two faults, one limit that no shape change can fix.

rim_r contradicted the measurements

The fit-check stand-in used an eyeballed 3.5 mm edge radius. That implies a 38 mm front face — too small to carry a 42 mm glass, so every interference check had been running against a shape that cannot exist. rim_r is now derived as (disc_d − glass_d)/2 = 1.5 mm. The seat still measures 0.00 mm³, and the shell stands 2.71 mm proud rather than the 1.75 previously quoted.

The bore clearance was left over from the wrong size

fit = 0.5 dated from when the shell was thought to be 40.5 mm. On a 45 mm bore it dies at the pessimistic end of FDM hole shrink — 0.5 mm of shrink leaves zero clearance and the shell will not go in at all. Now 0.8, which still enters with 0.3 mm to spare, and the extra slop only eats a 1.0 mm ledge.

It will slide when you swipe it

At 3 walls and 15% infill the part is ~12 g, ~42 g with the display in it. Bare PLA on a desk skids at about 31 g of finger force and a deliberate swipe is 100–300 g. Silicone feet raise that to ~163 g, and printing at 40–50% infill roughly doubles the part's mass again. This is mass, not geometry — no change to the shape fixes it.

Came back clean

Tipping — pressing the screen cannot tip it backwards at any force: at 38° the push's downward component acts on a 54 mm arm to the back edge while the backward component has only 28.9 mm, so it presses the stand down. Screen clearance — 5.3 mm radial from lip to active area. The USB-C notch takes 43.7° of ring, but at the side, while the in-plane load is carried at the bottom. Base floor under the pocket scoop bottoms out at 1.14 mm, six layers. Mesh watertight: zero naked and zero non-manifold edges.
