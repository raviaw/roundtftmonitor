This is the whole design in one view. The shell is a rounded pebble, so a plain bore would touch it on a single line and let it rock. Instead the bore is cut wider than the shell and a front lip closes in over it: the shell slides in from the back and stops when its face meets that lip. Because the cradle leans back 20°, gravity presses it onto the lip rather than out of it — no clip, no clamp, and it still lifts out by hand.

Design notes

Why it is shaped this way

A lip, not a cone

The first attempt seated the shell in a shallow cone, wedging it at its equator. That was abandoned: where a cone grips depends on how the shell's rim curves, which cannot be measured off a photograph, and the first test drove the shell 1208 mm³ into the cone wall. A lip does not care about the rim profile — it stops the shell in the same place whatever the rim does.

The USB-C exits sideways

The port is on the shell's rim, and a right-angle plug needs about 10 mm of radial room. At the bottom of the ring that room is below the desk, so the notch is cut through the side instead, where it costs nothing and leaves the load-bearing bottom arc of the ring intact. Rotate the UI in firmware to match.

Two faults the numbers caught

The interference check found what looking at renders did not. First, the base slab was filling the bottom of the pocket — the ring merges into the base, so the seat has to be cut after the base and leg are unioned on, not before. Second, the ring's back-bottom edge hung 1.31 mm below the build plate, so the part would not have sat flat.

Printing

Print base-down as oriented, in PLA or PETG, 3 perimeters and about 15% infill. The underside of the leaning cradle is a mild overhang — enable light supports touching the build plate only if your slicer flags it; the 20° lean keeps overhangs modest. A brim helps the small footprint stay put.
