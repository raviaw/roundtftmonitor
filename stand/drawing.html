This is the whole design in one view. The shell is a rounded pebble, so a plain bore would touch it on a single line and let it rock. Instead the bore is cut wider than the shell and a front lip closes in over it: the shell slides in from the back and stops when its face meets that lip. Because the cradle leans back 38°, gravity presses it onto the lip rather than out of it — no clip, no clamp, and it still lifts out by hand.

The tightest fit — lip against glass

Measured, 1.5 mm to play with

The shell is 45.0 mm across and its black glass is 42.0 mm, which leaves a plastic rim just 1.5 mm wide on radius. That rim is the only thing the lip can land on, and it is the tightest fit anywhere in the part. aperture splits it: 0.5 mm clear of the glass, 1.0 mm of ledge under the shell.

Holes print undersize on FDM, which is why the glass side gets the clearance and the ledge absorbs the error — a lip that comes out 0.3 mm tight still holds, a lip that creeps onto the glass makes the shell rock. stand.scad asserts the bore stays between the two, so the render fails rather than quietly producing a cradle that sits on the screen.

Design notes

Why it is shaped this way

A lip, not a cone

The first attempt seated the shell in a shallow cone, wedging it at its equator. That was abandoned: where a cone grips depends on how the shell's rim curves, which cannot be measured off a photograph, and the first test drove the shell 1208 mm³ into the cone wall. A lip does not care about the rim profile — it stops the shell in the same place whatever the rim does.

The USB-C exits sideways

The port is on the shell's rim, and a right-angle plug needs about 10 mm of radial room. At the bottom of the ring that room is below the desk, so the notch is cut through the side instead, where it costs nothing and leaves the load-bearing bottom arc of the ring intact. Rotate the UI in firmware to match.

Three faults the numbers caught

Measuring found what looking at renders did not. The base slab was filling the bottom of the pocket — the ring merges into the base, so the seat has to be cut after the base and leg are unioned on, not before. The ring's back-bottom edge hung 1.31 mm below the build plate. And the base plate itself was floating 2.25 mm off the plate, leaving the part balanced on an 18 × 8 mm bar at the back: the plate was being lifted by half its thickness as though it were centred on the origin, when it was already built up from it.

The stance is derived, not typed in

Lean the cradle further and its lowest point moves, which is how it ended up under the build plate the first time. So the cradle's height is now computed from the lean angle rather than written down beside it — change tilt alone and the geometry follows.

Printing

Print base-down as oriented, in PLA or PETG, 3 perimeters and about 15% infill. The underside of the leaning cradle is a mild overhang — enable light supports touching the build plate only if your slicer flags it; at 38° the underside is a real overhang, though still inside the 45° most printers manage unsupported. A brim helps the small footprint stay put.

Why no supports

Only 46 mm² of the whole part leans past 55°, and it is all one feature: the lead-in chamfer at the top-back of the ring. That chamfer is cut at 45° to the ring's axis, so once the ring leans 38° it ends up facing nearly straight down. It will droop a little, which costs nothing — the chamfer exists only to let the shell start into the bore. Supports would scar the seat instead.

Slow the layers down

The stock profiles are built for speed and this part has a tiny cross-section per layer — up in the ring, each layer is a thin arc. At full speed those layers finish before the one beneath has set and the ring comes out soft and glossy. A minimum layer time of 8–10 s, or capping the outer wall to about 30% speed, fixes it. Printing PLA in an enclosed machine, leave the door and top open so the chamber doesn't heat-soak the extruder.
